# The invisible triangle

Hero's _Dioptra_ 15 asks for a straight excavation through a mountain when the
two mouths are given. A circuit of right angles determines the components of
the hidden connecting line. Small similar triangles outside the mountain then
establish collinear aiming lines at the two entrances.

The source is Hermann Schöne's 1903 edition, printed pp. 238–241, including
figure 95 and the facing German translation and apparatus. The Greek text is
also available in the Harvard College Library / Open Greek and Latin TEI
edition, `tlg0559.tlg007.1st1K-grc1`, under CC BY-SA 4.0. Source identities and
adaptations are recorded separately. The interactive coordinates are new.

## Coordinate convention

Positive x is right and positive y is up on the unrotated plan. The portals
are B=(0,0), D=(X,Y), labelled Β and Δ. The measured circuit is B-E-Z-H-Θ-K-M-D.
Successive legs are horizontal or vertical. The signed x distances telescope
to X; the signed y distances telescope to Y. Adding every distance without a
sign gives the much longer circuit, not the hidden line.

The default uses X=300 and Y=60 chosen metres. Only its five-to-one ratio is
taken from Hero's numerical example. The book's figure is schematic; its drawn
dimensions do not reproduce that ratio. Two- and seven-to-one plans are modern
exercises. The mountain outline is synthetic and represents no elevation map.

Moving the circuit's bends keeps both portals fixed. H moves farther right,
while intermediate stations also move. Total route length changes from 560 to
760 metres in the default case; the net displacement remains (300,60).

For a chosen outside baseline b, the perpendicular aiming offset is bY/X.
The external points are Xi=(-b,-bY/X) and Pi=(X+b,Y+bY/X). Extending Xi-B and
Pi-D inward gives the same line. Coordinates, similar triangles and additions
are sufficient; the modern angle/length readout is explanatory, not a claim
that Hero used decimal degrees or the displayed numerical procedure.

## A source-label difficulty

The construction moves the instrument along K-L until D is seen at right
angles from M. The printed summation lists subsequently use K-L and L-D,
while the diagram and preceding perpendicular construction identify M as the
closing station. This model sums the actually constructed K-M and M-D legs.
The full printed text, diagram and facing apparatus remain available. This
is an explicit geometric reading decision, not a silent correction of the
Greek or a claim to reconstruct an earlier manuscript.

## Chosen aiming error

The learner can displace either or both perpendicular pegs by a signed number
of modern millimetres, after choosing an aiming ratio a. For positive error e,
each outside peg moves farther from its baseline. The B-side slope is a+eB/b;
the D-side slope is a+eD/b. At the middle vertical cross-section x=X/2 their
vertical separation is X*a-Y + X*(eB+eD)/(2b).

This is a deterministic perturbation of the aiming pegs only. It is not an
estimate of ancient instrument accuracy, a traverse-error simulation, a tunnel
construction schedule, or a geology/engineering model. The bore control advances
each line through a fraction of its half of the horizontal span. It does not
represent equal elapsed time or equal drilled distances. The displayed meeting
gap is vertical at that middle station, not a shortest-distance calculation.

The exercise makes no claim that Eupalinos used Hero's later-described method
at Samos. No measurement from the actual tunnel is used.
